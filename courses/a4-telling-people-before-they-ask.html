Learn Incident Response for Data Teams with Phoebe · Leader session 4 of 6

Telling people before they ask

The first person to describe an incident sets the story everyone else repeats. If that person is a regional manager asking why revenue doubled, or a vendor asking why their access vanished, you spend the rest of the incident correcting someone else's version. Tonight is about getting there first: one source of truth, a cadence each audience can rely on, three messages drafted before you need them, and a regulator's clock that belongs to the privacy partner, not to the data team.

Part 0

The comms lead's job, from the first hour

Session 3 gave the communication role an owner. Tonight that owner gets a job description. The comms lead does not fix anything and does not decide severity. They keep everyone who needs to know on a predictable clock, they say only what the incident record says, and they make sure the one clock the data team cannot move, the regulator's, is in the hands of the person who owns it from the first hour.

★ What you walk out with tonight. LiveSilence is also an update3 min▶

When people who depend on the data hear nothing, they do not assume all is well. They assume nobody knows, or that somebody knows and is not saying. Both guesses generate messages: a manager pings an analyst directly, a finance lead re-runs a report and gets a third number, an executive asks in a group chat and gets four answers. Every one of those side conversations is a version of the incident you did not write.

The fix is not more messages. It is fewer, from one person, on a clock the reader can predict. A reader who knows the next update arrives at a stated time stops asking in between.

Real world

Constructed Quarrybank example. On the bad load, the first message anyone outside the data team received was a regional sales head's email asking why revenue had doubled, 7 hours 10 minutes after the bad write. By then the sales email had already gone to 40 people. The data team's first message was a reply to a complaint, which meant it was written defensively, in a hurry, to one person.

Self-studyWhat the comms lead does not do2 min▶
  • Diagnose. The comms lead reports what operations have confirmed. A theory heard on the call is not news until the incident commander records it.
  • Negotiate deadlines. Regulator notice belongs to the privacy partner, contract notice to whoever owns the contract. The comms lead makes sure each owner has the facts in time.
  • Write the after-action report. The write-up belongs to the planned runbooks and postmortems course (Learn with Phoebe hub ↗). The comms lead's messages are an input to it, timestamped in the record.
Part 1 · covers update cadence by severity and NIST RS.CO-02

Who hears what, and on whose clock

6 min live

NIST's revised incident guidance asks for procedures that "include what must be reported to whom and at what times (e.g., initial notification, regular status updates)". Three nouns hide in that sentence: what, whom, when. Most teams write down the first and improvise the other two during the incident, which is the worst moment to decide who counts as an audience.

LiveWhat NIST and the SRE book ask of the communicator3 min▶

NIST SP 800-61 Revision 3 files communication under RS.CO-02, and its recommendation is procedural rather than stylistic: the plan says "what must be reported to whom and at what times (e.g., initial notification, regular status updates)". Two kinds of message are named there. The initial notification tells people an incident exists. Regular status updates keep telling them, on a schedule, whether or not anything changed.

Google's SRE book gives the role its identity. Communication is "the public face of the incident response task force", one of four roles alongside command, operations and planning. The word that matters is face, singular. If three engineers each post their own update, the organisation has three faces and the readers pick whichever one frightens them least.

Write the status block first, then the message. The comms lead copies from the record, not from the call. If the record does not say it, the message does not say it, and the fix for a thin message is a better record.

LiveA cadence by severity, written as policy3 min▶

Quarrybank's cadence below is a constructed policy choice, not a published standard. The principle behind it is practice: the higher the severity, the shorter the gap between updates, and every update states when the next one comes, even if the next one will say "no change".

SeverityIncident channelExecutivesOutside parties
SEV-1Every 30 minutes, and on any changeEvery 2 hours, and on severity changeWhen reach is confirmed, then daily
SEV-2Every hourAt declare and at close, and on severity changeWhen reach is confirmed
SEV-3At declare, on change, at closeWeekly summary onlyOnly if a delivered output was affected

One row overrides the table: any exposure of personal data pages the privacy partner within the first hour, whatever severity it was given. Session 2's matrix should already rate that case high; the override exists for the night it does not.

Real world

Constructed Quarrybank example. The board deck incident has the slowest clock of the three: the finance reconciliation flagged it 48 hours after the margin figure went into the board pack. Quarrybank's policy says the audit committee chair is told within 48 hours of anyone becoming aware of a misstated board figure. That is an executive audience with a clock set by internal policy, and the comms lead has to know it exists before the incident, not discover it in week two.

Part 2 · covers the holding statement, the executive update and the partner note

Three messages, drafted while nothing is on fire

5 min live

Every data incident needs some version of the same three messages: one that says an incident exists, one that tells leadership what it means for them, and one that asks somebody outside to do something. All three are hard to write well at 3am and easy to write well on a Tuesday afternoon. Draft them now, with blanks, and the incident fills in the blanks.

LiveThe holding statement: known, not known, next2 min▶

A holding statement is the first message after declaring, and it has one job: to occupy the silence honestly before anyone fills it with a guess. It says three things and nothing else.

  1. What you know, stated as observation: which data, since when, which outputs are affected so far.
  2. What you do not know yet, said plainly. "We do not yet know whether the forecast model read the bad rows" is more reassuring than silence, because it shows somebody is looking.
  3. When the next update comes, as a time, not as "soon".

It must never contain a cause you have not confirmed. The first cause anyone writes down is the one everybody remembers, and in data incidents the first theory is often a symptom: the supplier feed duplicated a partition, but the reason the duplicate was loaded at all may sit in your own pipeline. A guessed cause in writing also lands in the record, where it reads as a finding to a regulator, an auditor or a partner's lawyer.

LiveThe executive update and the partner note3 min▶

The executive update is the holding statement plus consequence. Leaders do not need the mechanism; they need to know which of their decisions, numbers or relationships are touched, and whether anything is being asked of them. PagerDuty's role list names both an Internal Liaison and a Customer Liaison, and the split is a useful habit: the people inside and the people outside need different messages built from the same facts.

The partner note goes outside, so it carries an ask, not just news. For a quality incident the ask is usually "stop using the extract you received on [date], we will resend it corrected". For an exposure it is "stop using, delete every copy, confirm in writing who accessed it". A partner note is reviewed by the privacy partner before it leaves whenever personal data is involved, because what you write to an outside party can become evidence.

Real world

Constructed Quarrybank example, measured on the bench. The partner contract says the supplier partner is told within 24 hours of Quarrybank becoming aware of an error in a delivered extract; on the bad load that is due 24h40 after the bad write. With a declare rule only, and even with roles, the partner is never told and the contract notice is missed: the team does not know the extract read the bad rows. Only lineage-driven notice, the responder track's session 6, gets the partner told 2h00 after the bad write. A template is necessary; it is not sufficient if you do not know who to send it to.

Part 3 · covers regulator notice as a deadline owned by the privacy partner

The regulator's clock is an input, not a debate

4 min live

The leaked table carries personal data, so two clocks start that the data team does not set and cannot negotiate. What triggers them, what the notice must contain and whether it is needed at all are legal questions, owned by the privacy partner and taught in the GDPR and PDPA courses. The data team's job is narrower and more urgent: make sure the person who owns those clocks hears about the incident while there is still time on them.

LiveTwo clocks you do not set2 min▶

Under GDPR Article 33 a controller notifies the supervisory authority "without undue delay and, where feasible, not later than 72 hours after having become aware of it". Under Singapore's PDPA, as reported, the organisation notifies the PDPC no later than 3 calendar days after determining that the breach is notifiable. Both are fixed inputs to Quarrybank's plan. What counts as becoming aware, what counts as notifiable and what the notice must say belong to the courses that own the law: GDPR breach response ↗ and PDPA breach response ↗.

The data team's contribution is time. Every hour between the signal and the privacy partner hearing about it is an hour taken off a clock the privacy partner then has to work inside. That is why the comms lead's first call on an exposure goes to legal, not to leadership.

LiveWhat changed between R1 and R22 min▶

The bench models plan times by stated rules, and the rule that moves here is the formal notice. With a declare rule but no roles, nobody owns the regulator notice, so it waits on the slowest path: the bench models it as landing 120 hours after the declaration. With roles, the privacy partner is in the room from assessment, and the notice follows assessment by 24 hours. Measured from the bad write, that is 123h35 against 27h55.

Real world

Constructed Quarrybank example, measured on the bench. On R1 the leaked table's "what it reached" panel reads: GDPR due 75h20 after the bad write, done 123h35, missed; PDPA due 87h00, done 123h35, missed. On R2 the due times are identical and the done time is 27h55 for both, met. The law did not get kinder and the team did not type faster. The person who owns the clock was told in the first hour.

Self-studySEC Form 8-K Item 1.05, the board-level analogue3 min▶

US-listed companies have a disclosure clock of their own. Form 8-K Item 1.05, adopted in 2023, is due "four business days after a registrant determines that a cybersecurity incident is material", and describes the "material aspects of the incident's nature, scope, and timing" and its material impact. It applies to US registrants and to cybersecurity incidents only. Quarrybank is not a registrant, and a margin error is not a cybersecurity incident, so it does not apply here.

It is worth knowing as a shape. The clock starts at a determination, not at the event, which puts a premium on how fast the determination is made, and the determination is made by leadership with the board watching. That is the same shape as Quarrybank's internal 48-hour chair notice, and the subject of session 5.

Four claims, none confirmed. A cause, a culprit, a fix and an impact assessment, all in one line, all before anyone has read the access log. Each one is now in writing, and each one has to be walked back in front of the people who read it.

Homework

Before session 5

30 min
  1. Fill in the cadence table for your own organisation. For each severity, write who hears, how often, and who sends it. Show it to one executive and ask whether the gap between updates is one they would tolerate.
  2. Find the last message your team sent during a data problem. Mark every sentence as known, not known, doing or next. Anything that fits none of them, especially a cause, comes out of your template.
  3. Ask your privacy partner how they would like to be told about a possible exposure at 3am: channel, phone, email, deputy. Write it on page 4 next to "privacy partner, 1st hour".
  4. List every outside party that receives data from your team on a schedule: partners, vendors, regulators, auditors. For each one, find who owns the contract and whether it has a notice clause.
Check yourself

Three questions

What must a holding statement never contain?

A holding statement says what you know, what you do not, and when the next update comes. The first cause in writing is the one people remember and the one that lands in the record, so it waits until operations have confirmed it.

Who owns the regulator's notice clock for Quarrybank's leaked table?

GDPR's 72 hours and PDPA's 3 calendar days are fixed inputs owned by the privacy partner. The comms lead and the commander affect how much of that clock is left when the privacy partner is told.

On the bench, what changed for the leaked table's deadlines between R1 and R2?

Both rungs declare at 0h15 after the signal. R2 adds roles, so the privacy partner is in the room from assessment and the notice follows 24 hours later instead of waiting 120 hours on nobody.
