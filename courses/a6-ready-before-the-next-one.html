LiveWhy the last page is about practice3 min▶

Every page so far answers "what do we do". This page answers "how do we know we can". The two fail differently. A missing rule shows up on paper. A rule nobody can find at 3am, a role nobody accepts, a template nobody knows exists: those only show up when people try to use them under a clock.

So the readiness plan has three parts: a rehearsal on a date, a set of numbers that say whether the rehearsal went well, and a name that signs for both. Without the date the rehearsal drifts. Without the numbers it becomes a pleasant meeting. Without the name nobody notices when it stops happening.

Real world

Constructed Quarrybank example. After the third incident of the quarter the head of data asked a simple question in the review: if the bad load landed again tonight, who would declare it? Three people answered with three different names. Pages 1 to 5 existed. Nobody had walked through them together.

Self-studyWhere tonight sits in the whole plan2 min▶

Quarrybank's incident response plan has two halves. The leader half (six pages, this track) decides when to declare, how bad it is, who runs the room, who is told, what happens to a number the board used, and how the plan is kept ready. The responder half (seven pages, the responder track) is the hands-on work: the timeline, the consumer trace, the playbooks, the correction check, the notice and the close-out record. Part 1 · covers the tabletop exercise and NIST's continuous improvement

Rehearse on paper, with the real people

5 min live

A tabletop is an incident run in a meeting room. A facilitator hands out the events one card at a time, in the order they happened, and each person in their real role says out loud what they would do and when. A scribe writes the time against every move. Nothing is touched in production. What you get is the plan's first contact with the people who have to run it, at the price of an hour.

LiveHow to run one in an hour3 min▶

This is practice, not a published method: the shape most teams arrive at after a few attempts.

  1. Pick one real incident. A Quarrybank one for the first run, then your own. Real beats invented because people already half remember it.
  2. Seat the real roles. The person actually on call next week, the head of data, the privacy partner, the finance controller. Not their deputies, and not the person who wrote the plan.
  3. Inject in order, one card at a time. The signal first. Do not show the next card until the room has said what it does with this one.
  4. Make people say the move and the time. "I would look into it" is not a move. "I declare in #incident at 0h50 and name myself owner" is.
  5. The scribe writes every time. Those times are the output. Everything else is conversation.
  6. Stop at the first broken page. When nobody can find the severity matrix, that is the finding. Write it down and move to the next card.
LiveNIST: lessons do not wait for the end2 min▶

NIST SP 800-61 Revision 3 (April 2025) replaced its old four-phase life cycle, which ended with post-incident activity, with a model built on the six CSF 2.0 Functions. Improvement, ID.IM, now feeds lessons back at all times rather than at the end. The guide gives its reason plainly: "Today, incidents occur frequently and cause far more damage." And on lessons learned, they "should often be shared as soon as they are identified, not delayed until after recovery concludes."

A tabletop is that idea applied before any incident at all. You identify the lesson in a meeting room on a quiet Thursday, and the page is fixed before it is needed. NIST is written for cybersecurity incidents; the improvement loop carries over to quality and decision incidents unchanged.

Real world

Constructed Quarrybank example. At Quarrybank's first tabletop the on-call engineer took card 1 and said she would keep an eye on it. Nobody in the room had read page 1. The scribe's first time went in against card 3, at the complaint. That is exactly how the no-plan run on the bench behaves, and it took 40 minutes to find out on paper instead of a quarter to find out in production.

Self-studyWhat a tabletop cannot tell you2 min▶
  • Whether the tools work. Saying "I pause the load" in a room is not pausing the load. The responder track's playbooks are tested against systems, not slides.
  • How tired people behave. A room at 10am is calmer than a call at 3am. Expect real times to be slower than tabletop times, and set targets with that margin in mind.
  • Whether detection will fire. The facilitator hands out the signal card. In real life someone has to build the monitor that produces it, which is the observability side (Data Observability ↗).
Part 2 · covers three readiness numbers and the data downtime framing

Three numbers, all timed from the first signal

5 min live

A board cannot read a plan. It can read three numbers. Time to declare: from the first signal to somebody saying the word. Time to contain: from the first signal to the bad data stopping spreading or the door closing. Time to correct: from the first signal to every consumer reading correct rows. All three start at the first signal, because that is the earliest moment the response could have started, and every minute after it belongs to the plan.

Measuring from the first signal is a choice with a reason. The bad write happened earlier, but nothing a response plan does can act before somebody could have known. Starting the clock at the signal makes the numbers belong to the plan and not to the monitor. The other choice, from the bad write, is what the bench uses for deadlines due and done, because a regulator's or partner's clock does not care when you noticed. Say which one you mean every time you quote a number.

LiveWhat rehearsal buys, and what it cannot3 min▶

On the bench R4 is the full plan written down: declare rule, severity and roles, containment playbook, lineage-driven notice. R5 is the same plan, rehearsed. The bench models plan times by stated rules: rehearsal declares 5 minutes after the signal instead of 15, assesses faster, contains faster and corrects faster. The three readiness numbers fall from 0h15 / 0h50 / 6h50 to 0h05 / 0h25 / 3h25.

The headline does not move. Consumers and decisions on bad data stay at 5 + 1, never told stays at 0, deadlines met stay at 4 of 4. R4 already reached the floor: the 5 + 1 that remain were reached before the first signal, so no faster response can reach them first. Rehearsal buys time, not reach.

Real world

Constructed Quarrybank example, measured on the bench. The one number that does move besides time is the leaked table: rows exposed fall from 8,400 under the written plan to 7,600 rehearsed, because the doors close sooner and the vendor reads less in between. Time and rows exposed are what a rehearsal shows up in. A board that asks why the headline stayed at 5 + 1 is asking the right question, and the answer is detection, not response.

Self-studyThe data downtime framing, taught as a claim2 min▶

Barr Moses of Monte Carlo, a data observability vendor, defines data downtime as "periods of time when your data is partial, erroneous, missing or otherwise inaccurate" and gives it as "Number of incidents x (average time to detection + average time to resolution)".

It is a vendor's framing, written to make a case for observability tooling, and it is useful on its own terms: it splits the cost of a bad table into a detection half and a resolution half. The three readiness numbers live inside the resolution half. The detection half, from the bad write to the first signal, is the observability course's side of the seam, and it is the half that moves the 5 + 1. Read the formula as a way to argue, not as a measured law; no public base rate for data incident frequency was found to put into it.

Part 3 · covers the one-page plan, the hand-offs and the floor

One page, signed, and what it hands on

5 min live

Six leader pages become one page the on-call engineer can read at 3am, each page reduced to the line that matters. Around it sit three things the plan does not own and should say so: the write-up after an incident, the evidence package, and the consumers who were reached before anybody could have known.

LiveWhy one page, and why signed2 min▶

The full pages stay in the folder for the people who need detail. The one page is what goes on the on-call laptop, in the incident channel's pinned message and on the back of the roles card. If the on-call engineer has to open six documents to find out whether to declare, the declare rule has quietly become "when somebody senior notices".

The signature is not ceremony. Pages 4 and 5 commit the privacy partner and the finance controller to clocks and decisions they own. A plan signed only by the data team binds only the data team. Quarrybank's leader half is signed by the head of data, who owns the plan, and the privacy partner, who owns the notice clocks it feeds.

LiveThree things the plan hands on3 min▶
  • The write-up. What happened, why, and what changes, written after the incident and read by people who were not on the call. That craft belongs to the planned runbooks and postmortems course; it is not yet live, so follow it from the Learn with Phoebe hub ↗. The plan's job is to make sure the timeline and decisions it needs were written down at the time.
  • The incident package. The evidence bundle a reliability team assembles for an incident: owned by reliability (Reliability, the incident package ↗).
  • The floor. On the bench, 5 consumers + 1 decision survive any response plan, because they were reached before the first signal: the vendor's early download and the unknown address on the leaked table, and the board pack read, with the "Hold prices in two regions" decision made at 24h00, before the 48h00 signal. The only lever left is earlier detection, which is the observability side (Data Observability ↗).
Real world

Constructed Quarrybank example, measured on the bench. When the head of data showed the board the rehearsed plan, the chair asked why the headline was still 5 + 1 after six pages of work. The honest answer took one sentence: those five consumers and one decision were reached before any alarm fired, so the next investment is a monitor, not another page.

Self-studyKeeping the plan alive between incidents2 min▶

Practice, not a finding: plans decay through people moving, not through rules aging. Tie the plan's review to events that already happen. When the on-call rota changes, the new person reads the one page (Reliability, on-call rota ↗). When a privacy partner or finance controller changes, the plan is re-signed. When a real incident closes, its scribe's times go next to the tabletop times on page 6, and any page that slowed the response gets an owner and a date, in line with NIST's advice not to wait for recovery to share a lesson.

Build-along

Read the clock three ways, then sign page 6

★ 22 min · everyone builds

No code. Read the three readiness numbers for every incident on no plan, the written plan and the rehearsed plan. Turn them into board targets, put the next tabletop in a calendar, write page 6, and assemble the leader half of Quarrybank's plan.

Read R0, no plan. In the bench table, read time to declare, contain and correct for each incident. They are measured from the first signal. Write all nine. Notice that the board deck's 120h00 to declare is mostly waiting for the external auditor's question.

Switch to R4, the full written plan. All three incidents now read 0h15 / 0h50 / 6h50. Read the headline too: 5 consumers + 1 decision on bad data, 0 never told, 4 of 4 deadlines met. This is the floor.

Switch to R5, rehearsed. The times fall to 0h05 / 0h25 / 3h25. The headline stays at 5 + 1. Find the one other number that moves: leaked rows exposed, 8,400 on R4 and 7,600 on R5. Write one sentence for the board explaining why rehearsal moved the time and not the headline.

★ Step 3 · the board sentence, QuarrybankRehearsing the plan cut our time to correct from 6h50 to 3h25 after the first signal, and the leaked table exposed 7,600 rows instead of 8,400. It does not change the 5 consumers and 1 decision reached before any alarm fired: only earlier detection can, and that is next year's ask.

Set the targets and book the next tabletop. Decide what you will report to the board each quarter: the three numbers, current against target. Quarrybank takes R4 as the committed target and R5 as the stretch. Then put the next tabletop in the calendar before you leave, with a facilitator who did not write the plan.

✕ The readiness plan most teams actually haveWe will review the incident process annually and update it as needed.

"As needed" means after the next incident. A review with no date, no numbers and no name is a review that happens when something has already gone wrong. A tabletop on a date, timed by a scribe, is the review happening first.

Homework

Before the next one

60 min
  1. Run a tabletop on one incident, Quarrybank's or your own, with the real roles and a scribe. Keep the scribe's times.
  2. Take your last real incident and measure the three numbers from its first signal. If nobody knows when the first signal was, that is your first finding.
  3. Put the next tabletop in the calendars of everyone who signs page 6, with a named facilitator.
  4. Find out who in your organisation will own incident write-ups, and write their name under page 6 next to "lessons".
Check yourself

Three questions

On the bench, what does rehearsing the plan (R5) change compared with the written plan (R4)?

R4 already reached the floor: the 5 + 1 were reached before the first signal. Rehearsal buys time and fewer rows exposed, not reach. Only earlier detection moves the headline.

What are the three readiness numbers, and where are they measured from?

The first signal is the earliest moment a response plan could act, so starting there makes the numbers belong to the plan. Deadlines are a different clock: those run from the bad write.

Who owns the write-up after an incident closes?

The leader half decides when, how bad, who and what is said. Writing the after-incident story well is its own craft, handed on by name; the evidence package belongs to reliability.
