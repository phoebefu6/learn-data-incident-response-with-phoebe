Severity you can defend

A declared incident still needs a size. Too low and the people who could stop the spread are asleep; too high and the whole organisation learns to ignore the pager. Tonight builds a severity matrix from three things you can check in minutes, reach, sensitivity and reversibility, rates Quarrybank's three incidents with it, and then holds your rating up against the reach the bench actually computes.

Part 0

Where severity sits in the response

Session 1 wrote the declare rule: any yes to three questions, and somebody names the incident within 15 minutes of the signal. That rule is deliberately cheap to fire, which means it will fire on small things. Severity is what makes that safe. It is the second decision, taken minutes after the first, and it decides how many people the declaration wakes, how fast, and who has to be in the room before anyone touches the data.

Live = presented in the 45 minutes Self-study = the full depth, after class 🔍 Every diagram zooms

★ What you walk out with tonight. A severity matrix that two leaders, rating the same incident separately, land on the same level with, plus a ladder of who is woken at each level. That is page 2 of Quarrybank's incident response plan. On the bench, severity and roles together move Quarrybank's quarter from 0 of 4 deadlines met to 3 of 4.

LiveWhy "defend" and not "decide"3 min▶

Every severity call gets questioned later: by the person who was woken at 2am for something that turned out small, by the executive who heard about a large one from a customer, by an auditor reading the timeline. A rating you can defend is one where you can point at the criteria and show which box the incident was in at the moment you rated it. A rating you merely decided is one where the only answer is "it felt serious".

The test is simple. Hand the same incident to two leaders who have not spoken to each other. If they come back with different levels, the matrix is the problem, not the leaders.

Real world

Constructed Quarrybank example. When the leaked customer table was finally picked up, the head of data called it "a sharing mistake, low" because every row was correct. The privacy partner called it the worst of the quarter because 18,400 rows of contact details and credit limits had left the building. Both were describing the same facts. Neither had a matrix, so the argument took the first hour.

Self-studyWhat severity is not2 min▶
  • Not a judgement on the person who caused it. A SEV-1 caused by a one-character typo is still a SEV-1. Tie the level to the data, never to the mistake.
  • Not a legal classification. Whether the leaked table must be reported to a regulator is the privacy partner's question, with its own clock. Severity makes sure the privacy partner is in the room in time to answer it.
  • Not fixed. Reach grows while an incident is open. Re-rate whenever a new consumer, a new outside party or a new copy turns up, and say so in the record.
Part 1 · covers PagerDuty severity levels and NIST RS.MA prioritisation

Three axes that push a rating up

6 min live

Two published sources set the frame. PagerDuty's incident response documentation gives the levels and the habits around them. NIST SP 800-61 Revision 3 gives the reason to rate at all: incidents "should not be handled on a first-come, first-served basis". A data team then needs criteria it can check in minutes. Quarrybank uses three: how far the data has reached, how sensitive it is, and whether a correction can undo the reading.

LivePagerDuty's five levels, and two habits worth copying3 min▶

PagerDuty publishes the severity scale its own responders use. Read it for its shape, not its wording, because it is written for a product with customers who log in:

LevelPagerDuty's definitionData translation
SEV-1"Critical issue that warrants public notification and liaison with executive teams"Someone outside will have to be told; an executive has to know first
SEV-2"Critical system issue actively impacting many customers' ability to use the product"Many consumers are reading or acting on bad data now
SEV-3"Stability or minor customer-impacting issues that require immediate attention from service owners"A few consumers inside; the owners of those copies act today
SEV-4"Minor issues requiring action, but not affecting customer ability to use the product"Caught before anyone outside the team read it
SEV-5"Cosmetic issues or bugs"Usually not an incident for a data team at all; a ticket

Two habits sit next to the scale. First, if you are unsure between two levels, treat it as the higher one. Second: "During an incident is not the time to discuss or litigate severities". Pick, move, and argue about the level in the review afterwards, where the argument can improve the matrix instead of delaying the response.

Four levels are enough for most data teams. Quarrybank drops SEV-5: a cosmetic issue in a dashboard label goes to the backlog, not the incident channel. Fewer levels means fewer borderline calls at 2am.

LiveWhat NIST says to weigh, and why first-come is wrong3 min▶

NIST SP 800-61 Revision 3 calls evaluating risk and prioritising incidents "perhaps the most critical decision points" in the response, and recommends that "incidents should not be handled on a first-come, first-served basis". The factors it lists include "asset criticality, functional impact of the incident, data impact of the incident ... and recoverability".

Quarrybank's three axes are a data team's reading of that list, said out loud as a teaching construction rather than a standard:

  • Reach stands in for functional impact: how many consumers, people and rows, and whether any of them sit outside the company.
  • Sensitivity stands in for data impact and asset criticality: an internal working number, a number people act on, or personal data and a figure the board or a lender relied on.
  • Reversibility stands in for recoverability: whether fixing the table fixes what people read, or whether a copy now lives somewhere a correction cannot reach.
Real world

Constructed Quarrybank example. In the quarter the incidents arrived in order: bad load, leaked table, board deck. First-come would have put the board deck last. On the matrix it is SEV-1, level with the leak, because the figure had reached a board and, by the time anyone declared it, a lender.

Part 2 · covers reach as a number the bench computes

Reach is counted, not felt

6 min live

Of the three axes, reach is the one leaders guess worst, because the people who read a bad number rarely say so. The bench does not guess. It replays each incident's constructed event log and lists every consumer, decision and reader that touched the bad data before it was corrected or its readers were told. On R0, no plan at all, those lists are the honest size of each incident.

LiveThree incidents, three different kinds of big3 min▶

Read the panels across and the incidents stop looking comparable, which is the point of having three axes instead of one score.

  • The bad load has the most readers: 60 bad reads by 52 people, most of them on the daily sales email. Its decisions (a replenishment order, a promotion budget cut, the partner's rebate) are internal or commercial.
  • The leaked table has no bad reads at all, because every row is correct. Its size is 4 outside parties and 18,400 rows of contact details and credit limits. The unknown address can never be told.
  • The board deck has the fewest readers, 35 bad reads by 17 people, and the heaviest decisions: holding prices, an investor update, a covenant certificate, a bonus accrual.

A single "impact score" would rank the bad load first because it has the most readers. The matrix ranks it last of the three, because readers inside the company can be told and dashboards refresh.

Real world

Constructed Quarrybank example, measured on the bench. With no plan the quarter reaches 14 consumers and 7 decisions with bad data, and 11 consumers are never told. Every one of those 11 was reachable through the lists above; nobody had been given the job of reading them.

LiveReversibility: why a copy outranks a dashboard3 min▶

When the bad partition is corrected, the executive dashboard, the finance mart and the board portal pick up the corrected rows at their next refresh. Their readers saw a wrong number for a while; the next time they look, it is right.

Copies do not refresh. The daily sales email sits in 40 inboxes, the partner extract sits in the partner's systems, the forecast model has already scored with the doubled revenue, and the emailed pack, the pricing committee deck, the investor update and the lender certificate all say what they said on the day they were made. On the bench, a copy only clears when someone tells its users. That is why reversibility is an axis of its own: every copy outside the walls is a conversation somebody has to have, and nobody can have it on your behalf.

The quick reversibility question at rating time: "If we fixed the table this minute, who would still be holding the wrong number?" Every name in the answer is a copy.

Part 3 · covers who is woken at each level, and rating the same incident the same way

Each level wakes someone new

5 min live

A severity level is only useful if it changes what happens next. At Quarrybank each level adds people and never removes any: SEV-4 is the on-call engineer and a log line, SEV-1 has the privacy partner and the finance controller in the first hour. The ladder is written before the incident so that nobody has to decide, at 2am, whether this one is worth waking the head of data for.

LiveRate by impact now, or by worst plausible impact?3 min▶

This is the question two careful leaders most often split on, and there is no settled answer. Both positions are held by experienced teams.

  • Impact now. Rate what has happened: the consumers already reached, the copies already made. It is checkable, it gives two raters the same answer, and it avoids paging people for a scenario that never arrives. Re-rate as reach grows.
  • Worst plausible impact. Rate what will happen if nobody acts: the copies on the calendar, the parties the data is about to reach. It wakes the right people before the spread, which is when they are most useful, at the cost of more high-severity pages that turn out smaller.
Real world

Constructed Quarrybank example. When the finance reconciliation flagged the margin line at 48h00, the board deck had reached the board portal, the emailed pack and the pricing committee deck, all inside the company. By impact now it is SEV-2. But the investor update and the lender certificate were both still to be produced from the same pack, and by worst plausible impact it is SEV-1. On the bench, with no plan, both copies were made and both went outside.

Whichever you choose, write it on the plan page. A matrix that does not say which question it answers is the main reason two leaders rate the same incident differently.

Self-studyMaking two raters agree4 min▶
  • Criteria in nouns, not adjectives. "Any outside party" can be checked. "Significant external impact" cannot.
  • One level per axis, highest wins. Read each axis separately, then take the rule that applies. Do not average.
  • Say which question it answers. Impact now, or worst plausible. See the card above.
  • Calibrate on old incidents. Give two leaders three closed incidents from last year and the matrix, separately. Every disagreement points at a criterion that needs a sharper noun.
  • Re-rating is normal. Record the old level, the new level and the new fact that moved it. That line in the timeline is what makes the rating defensible afterwards.

Who fills the incident commander, scribe and liaison roles, and how a handoff works, is session 3. The rota that decides who is on call at all belongs to reliability (on-call is a design ↗). For the leaked table, whether and when to notify a regulator stays with the privacy partner (GDPR breach response ↗, PDPA breach response ↗). Severity only makes sure that person is woken in the first hour.

Build-along

Rate all three, then let the bench check you

★ 22 min · everyone builds

No code. Rate Quarrybank's three incidents on the matrix before you look at any numbers, open the bench's "what it reached" panel on R0 to check each rating against the computed reach, then switch to R2 and watch what severity and roles do to the deadlines. Finish with page 2 of the incident response plan.

Rate before you look. Close the bench panels. Using only the incident descriptions from session 1 and the three axes in Part 1, write a level for each axis and an overall SEV for each Quarrybank incident. Write down which question you answered: impact now, or worst plausible.

Mark every copy. In each panel, list the consumers that will not refresh when the table is corrected. Those set your reversibility column. If any of them sits outside the company, reversibility is at the top, whatever the row count.

Switch to R1, then R2. R1 is the declare rule from session 1: 10 consumers and 4 decisions on bad data, 7 never told, deadlines met 0 of 4. R2 adds severity and roles: 7 + 3 on bad data, 4 never told, deadlines met 3 of 4. Open the leaked table's panel on both and compare the formal notice against the GDPR and PDPA due times. The bench models severity and roles as one plan page, an assessment within 20 minutes of declaring and a formal notice within a day of assessing, so it cannot tell you how much of the gain came from the rating and how much from the roles. Note too what R2 does not fix: the partner contract notice stays missed, because nobody yet tells the consumers that never complained. That is responder session 6.

Swap with a neighbour. Give the person next to you your matrix and one incident from your own last quarter, without your rating. Compare levels. If they differ, find the criterion that let them differ and rewrite it as a noun.

✕ The severity policy most teams actually haveSEV-1: major business impact. SEV-2: significant impact. SEV-3: moderate impact. SEV-4: minor impact.

Four adjectives, four arguments. "Major" and "significant" are the same word to two tired people. "Any outside party" is not.

Homework

Before session 3

30 min
  1. Run the calibration test. Give two colleagues the same three closed incidents and your matrix, separately, and compare their levels. Sharpen every criterion they disagreed on.
  2. For your most-read dashboard, list every copy of its data that would not refresh if the source table were corrected tonight: scheduled emails, extracts, decks, models.
  3. Write your team's wake-up ladder by name, not by role, for this week. Mark any level where the named person has never been woken before.
  4. Decide, and write on your matrix, whether you rate by impact now or worst plausible impact. Bring one sentence on why.
Check yourself

Three questions

Twenty minutes into an incident, two leaders disagree on whether it is SEV-2 or SEV-1. What does PagerDuty's guidance say to do?

If unsure, treat it as the higher one. The argument about the level is worth having in the review, where it can sharpen the matrix, not on the call, where it delays everything else.

Which of Quarrybank's three incidents is hardest to reverse, and why?

Reversibility asks who still holds the data after the fix. Dashboards and the board portal refresh at the correction. A copy outside the walls only clears when someone tells its holder, and for the unknown address nobody can.

On the bench, what happens to deadlines met when Quarrybank adds severity and roles (R2) to the declare rule (R1)?

With someone owning the clock, the formal notices land within a day of assessing instead of days after declaring. The partner notice needs every reached consumer told, which arrives with lineage-driven notice later in the plan.
