Part 0

Contain first, because the fix takes hours and the spread takes minutes

A correct fix for a duplicated partition is real work: find the duplicate, rebuild the rows, rerun what depends on them, check the result. On the bench it lands hours after the first signal under every plan. Containment is different. It is a handful of switches that take minutes, and every one of them you flip early is a copy that never gets made. Tonight is about flipping them in the right order, before the fix, at 3am, without waiting for anyone's permission you could have arranged in advance.

LiveWhat containment means, in one sentence3 min▶

NIST SP 800-61 Rev. 3 puts it plainly under RS.MI-01: "Containment refers to preventing the expansion of an incident." Not undoing it, not fixing it. Preventing it from getting bigger. For a bad load, bigger means more copies: more tables built from the partition, more dashboards rendered from it, more files sent, more models trained on it, more people reading a number that is wrong.

That framing settles most 3am arguments. If an action makes the list of copies stop growing, it is containment and it goes first. If it makes the data right, it is the fix and it can wait for a quiet table.

Real world

Constructed Quarrybank example. The on-call engineer confirmed the duplicated partition within minutes of the 02:40Z alert and immediately started writing a deduplication query. Nobody paused the morning sales email, which went out on schedule to its 40 recipients with revenue doubled for two regions. The fix was correct. It was also late for everyone on that list.

Part 1 · covers containment as preventing expansion, NIST RS.MI-01

A valve on every outgoing edge

6 min live

Take the consumer trace from session 2 and look at it as a set of edges leaving the bad partition. Each edge has a mechanism that makes the next copy, a schedule, a query, an export, a job, and each mechanism has an off switch. Containment is putting your hand on every one of those switches. It does nothing to copies already made, and that limit is drawn in on purpose.

LiveThe four moves, and what each one stops3 min▶
MoveWhat you doWhat it stops
Pause downstream schedulesPause the orchestrator jobs that read the partition: mart refreshes, scheduled emails, report buildsThe next scheduled copy
Quarantine the partitionRevoke read on it, or move it out of reach of readers. Never delete it (responder session 1)Ad hoc queries and live dashboards
Circuit breakerA check on a job's input that stops the job when the input fails it, instead of running on bad rowsJobs you forgot to pause
Hold exportsStop any file, share or API push that leaves the warehouse for someone outside the teamCopies you cannot recall once sent

Pause, never cancel. A paused schedule remembers what it was going to do and resumes after the fix. A cancelled one has to be rebuilt from memory at the end of a long night, and that is where the second incident comes from.

LiveWhy exports go first2 min▶

Every copy inside the warehouse can be corrected by you later: rerun the mart, refresh the dashboard. A copy that has left the building, a partner file, an emailed report, can only be corrected by someone else, after you tell them. So the order of the valves follows the cost of the copy they stop: outgoing exports and sends first, then scheduled jobs, then the partition itself.

This is practitioner ordering, not a NIST rule. NIST tells you what containment is for; the order is how data teams make the most of the first minutes.

Self-studyCircuit breakers, built before the incident3 min▶

A circuit breaker is the only valve that closes itself. It is a check placed on a job's input, a row count range, a uniqueness test on the key, a freshness check, that makes the job stop instead of writing its output. During an incident it saves you from the job you forgot was downstream. Before an incident it is the difference between a bad load that spreads for an hour and one that stops at the first table.

Designing which checks catch a bad partition is detection work, and it belongs to observability. The incident question is simpler: which of your downstream jobs have a breaker, and which will happily run on whatever they are given? Write that list down before you need it.

Part 2 · covers what containment cannot do

No valve reaches a copy that has already left

5 min live

Containment works forward in time. It stops the next copy, not the last one. Any copy made before the valves closed stays wrong until someone corrects it or tells the people holding it, and the bench applies exactly that rule. That is why a much faster containment can leave the headline number unchanged and still be worth every minute of the drill.

LiveR2 to R3: what moved, and what did not3 min▶

R3 is R2 plus one thing: a containment playbook. The bench models plan times by stated rules, and with a playbook containment lands 15 minutes after assessment instead of 120. Measured from the first signal, containment moves from 2h35 to 0h50.

LiveSo why does R3 still matter?2 min▶

Because the headline is not the only number. The same playbook, applied to the leaked table, closes its doors sooner, and the bench measures 8,400 rows exposed instead of 12,800. Fewer customers' contact and credit-limit details were fetched before the doors closed. And the 0h50 is a capability: the next bad load may not have its worst copies made in the first 35 minutes.

Real world

Constructed Quarrybank example, measured on the bench. After the quarter's review, the head of data asked why the containment drill had not changed the headline. The answer the responder gave was the honest one: the two copies that count were made before anyone could know, so they are a notice problem, and session 6 deals with notice. The drill cut the leak's exposure from 12,800 rows to 8,400, and the next bad load gets the same 0h50.

Self-studyCopies already made become a notice list2 min▶

Every copy to the left of your valves goes into the record as its own row: what was copied, when, to whom, and who owns it. That list is the input to two later jobs, correcting the copies you control (session 5) and telling the owners of the ones you do not (session 6). On the bench, R2 and R3 leave the partner extract and the forecast model never told, and the partner contract notice missed. R4's lineage-driven notice is what clears them, not faster valves.

Keep the evidence while you build that list. NIST RS.AN-07 notes that collected incident data "is still considered evidence", so the quarantined partition, the job run ids and the export log are what prove which copies existed and when.

Part 3 · covers stopping the spread before the fix

Fix first, and the bad load keeps travelling while you work

5 min live

Every engineer's instinct is to fix the thing. It is also the fastest way to turn one incident into a bigger one, because a correct fix takes hours and nothing stops the copies while you write it. The bench has a trap for exactly this: T1, fix before you contain, which takes R4's plan and makes containment wait for the fix.

Measured on the benchR4 contain, then fixT1 fix before you contain

The correction lands at exactly the same moment. Everything T1 loses, it loses in the six hours between 0h50 and 6h50 when the valves could have been shut and were not. On the bad load alone, T1 reaches the sales email, sent 5h00 after the bad write to its 40 recipients, and the forecast model, and 24 people read bad data from it. On the leaked table, the doors stay open until the fix and all 18,400 rows are exposed.

Live"But the fix is nearly done"2 min▶

It always is, at 3am. Containment does not compete with the fix for the same person or the same hour: pausing a schedule and holding an export take minutes, and they make the fix safer, because you are no longer correcting a table that is being copied while you work. Contain, write it in the record, then fix on a quiet table.

Real world

Constructed Quarrybank example, measured on the bench. Under the fix-first plan, the deduplication query felt nearly ready an hour in, and the engineer kept going rather than stop to pause anything. The correction landed 6h50 after the signal, the same time as under the contain-first plan, and by then the sales email had gone out. 37 people read bad data instead of 13.

Self-studyWhere the fix itself is taught1 min▶

How to rebuild the partition and rerun what depends on it is backfill work, and it belongs to reliability: Learn Data Reliability, backfills ↗. Choosing between fixing forward and rolling back, and proving the correction reached every copy, is session 5. Tonight stops at the quiet table.

Build-along

Write the bad-load playbook

★ 22 min · everyone builds

You are on call at 02:40Z on 10 February and the partition is confirmed bad. Run the bench through four plans to see what a playbook buys and what fixing first costs, then write the first 15 minutes as a playbook anyone on the rota could follow alone. It becomes page 3 of the responder half of Quarrybank's plan.

Run R2, then R3, and read containment and leaked rows. In the bench table, read the contain column for each: 2h35 after the first signal under R2, 0h50 under R3. Then read rows exposed on the leaked table: 12,800, then 8,400. The headline stays at 7 + 3 on both. Say out loud why, using the partner extract and forecast model times from the bench's "what it reached" panel, which are measured from the bad write.

Run R4, then T1, and read the headline and the people count. R4 shows 5 + 1 and 13 people who read bad data. T1, the same plan with containment waiting for the fix, shows 7 + 1 and 37. Find the sales email in T1's reached list and note when it went out relative to the bad write.

★ Step 2 · what to write in your notesR2 contained 2h35 after signal leaked rows 12,800 headline 7 + 3 people 17 R3 contained 0h50 after signal leaked rows 8,400 headline 7 + 3 people 17 R4 contained 0h50, fixed 6h50 leaked rows 8,400 headline 5 + 1 people 13 T1 contained 6h50 = fixed 6h50 leaked rows 18,400 headline 7 + 1 people 37 Lesson: a faster valve cannot reach copies made before the signal. Lesson: waiting for the fix lets the bad load reach the sales email.

List your valves. Take your consumer trace from session 2 and write, for each edge, the switch that stops it: pause, quarantine, breaker or hold. Any edge with no switch is a gap to close before the next incident, not during it.

Decide who may flip each switch at 3am. A playbook step that needs a manager's approval at night does not run at night. For each step, either the on-call engineer is allowed to do it alone, agreed in advance and written down, or the step names who is called and how.

Write the playbook. Five steps in order, each with who may do it and what goes in the record. Strip Quarrybank's specifics where they would not survive the next incident and keep the shape.

★ Step 5 · plan, responder page 3, bad-load playbookQUARRYBANK INCIDENT RESPONSE PLAN · responder page 3 · bad-load playbook Use when a loaded partition is confirmed wrong. Contain before any fix. FIRST 15 MINUTES, in order: 1 Hold every outgoing export and send (partner extract, scheduled emails, API pushes). Who: on-call, alone. Record: each hold, with run id. 2 Pause every downstream schedule on the consumer trace. Pause, never cancel. Who: on-call, alone. Record: job names paused. 3 Quarantine the partition: revoke read or move it out of reach. Never delete. Who: on-call, permission granted in advance. Record: where it is, who can read it. 4 Check which circuit breakers tripped. List every copy made before step 1. Who: on-call. Record: one row per copy, with what, when (UTC), owner. 5 Tell the incident commander that containment is in place, and hand over the copy list. Who: on-call to commander. Commander decides the fix and the notices. THEN: fix on the quiet table (responder page 5 owns the correction check). Rule: copies made before containment stay wrong until corrected or told.

Rehearse the permissions, not just the steps. The most common reason a playbook step does not run at 3am is that the person on call does not have the grant to pause a job they do not own. Test every switch on a harmless table during the day.

Check yourself

Three questions

You have paused every schedule and quarantined the partition. What has that done to the partner extract that went out 0h30 after the bad write?

NIST RS.MI-01: containment prevents expansion. A copy made before the valves closed is outside their reach; it becomes a row on the notice list.

On the bench, T1 and R4 both land the correction 6h50 after the first signal. Why does T1 end with 37 people who read bad data instead of 13?

Same fix, same time; only the order differs. Under T1 the valves stay open for six hours longer, so the sales email to 40 recipients goes out and the leaked table exposes all 18,400 rows.

R3 adds a containment playbook to R2, and the headline stays at 7 + 3. Why does R3 still matter?

The headline counts copies made before the 0h40 signal, which no containment can reach. The playbook still cuts the leak from 12,800 exposed rows to 8,400 and gives a 0h50 containment for the next incident.
