The timeline starts at the first signal

When the monitor fires at 02:40 you are the only person awake, and the most valuable thing you can do in the next five minutes is not the fix. It is opening a record that says what you saw and when, in a clock everyone shares, and making sure the evidence is still there in the morning. Tonight you open that record for Quarrybank's bad load, learn what a scribe writes down and why it is written in UTC, and see on the bench why dropping the bad table to make the problem go away is the most expensive fast move there is.

LiveWhy the record comes before the fix3 min▶

Google's SRE book is direct about it: "The incident commander's most important responsibility is to keep a living incident document." Not the fix, the document. The fix ends the incident; the document is what lets anyone else join it, take it over, explain it to the board, or learn from it.

It also has a second job that data teams underrate. Every question you will be asked later, by the head of data, the privacy partner, the auditor or a regulator, is a question about time: when did you know, when did you act, who had read it by then. A record opened at the first signal answers all of them. A record reconstructed from chat history the next day answers them badly and in the wrong time zone.

Real world

Constructed Quarrybank example. The bad load's volume monitor fired at 02:40 UTC. The on-call engineer spent the first 40 minutes investigating in a direct message to a colleague. When the incident was finally declared, nobody could say from the record when the partner extract had gone out relative to the alert, which was exactly the question the partner's contract cared about.

Self-studyWho reads the record, and when3 min▶
  • In the first hour: the next person to join. The record is their briefing, so the current status sits at the top.
  • At a handover: the incoming commander. The SRE book has the outgoing one stay until the incoming one says yes.
  • Within days: the privacy partner, who needs the time you became aware, and the finance controller, who needs which figures were touched.
  • Weeks later: whoever writes the postmortem. That write-up is the docs course's craft; the raw material is your record.
Part 1 · covers the living incident document and the scribe role

One record, written as it happens

6 min live

The record is one document per incident, editable by everyone on the response, with the current state at the top and an append-only timeline underneath. PagerDuty's response guide names the person who keeps it: "A Scribe documents the timeline of an incident as it progresses." On a small team at 02:40, the scribe is you.

LiveWhat goes in a timeline row3 min▶

Six fields, every row, no exceptions. A row missing one of them is a row someone will have to re-investigate.

LiveThe status block, rewritten every update3 min▶

The SRE book asks for the most important information at the top of the living document. In practice that is a five-line block the commander rewrites, while the timeline below only grows: type, severity, owner, what is known now, and when the next update is due. Someone who reads only the block should be able to answer the head of data's question correctly.

Never edit a timeline row. If an entry was wrong, add a new row that corrects it. The record is evidence, and evidence that changes quietly stops being evidence.

Part 2 · covers timestamps that survive a merge

Every entry in UTC, because the evidence already is

5 min live

Warehouse logs, orchestrator runs and access logs are usually stamped in UTC. Chat messages, emails and people's memories are stamped in whatever zone the person was in. A timeline built from both without converting puts events in the wrong order, and the order is the whole point.

LiveThree habits that keep the clock honest3 min▶
  • Write the Z. 02:40Z, not 02:40. The suffix costs one character and ends the argument.
  • Take machine times from the machine. The query id, the run id or the log line carries its own timestamp. Copy it, do not retype it from memory.
  • Note when you learned something, not just when it happened. "02:30Z extract sent, learned at 03:10Z" is two facts, and the privacy partner and the partner contract both care about the second one.
Self-studyWhy "when did you become aware" is a timeline question2 min▶

Some notice deadlines run from the moment an organisation became aware, and the GDPR's 72 hours is the best known. Whether a given moment counts as awareness is a legal judgement that belongs to the privacy partner and the GDPR course ↗. Your job is narrower and more mechanical: make sure the record holds every candidate moment, first signal, first human look, declaration, with a UTC time and its proof, so that the person making that judgement is not guessing.

Part 3 · covers preserving evidence, NIST RS.AN-06 and RS.AN-07

Do not drop the bad table

5 min live

The fastest way to stop a bad table being read is to delete it, and at 02:45 it feels responsible. It destroys the only record of which rows were wrong and who read them. NIST asks that incident data be collected with "integrity and provenance" preserved, and notes that collected data "is still considered evidence" even when nobody expects a prosecution.

LiveQuarantine, snapshot, then fix3 min▶
  1. Quarantine: move or rename the bad partition out of reach of every reader, or revoke read on it. Readers stop; the rows stay.
  2. Snapshot: copy the bad rows, the load log and the access history for the window to a protected location, and write its path in the record.
  3. Then fix, in a new partition or table, so the before and after both exist. Session 5 covers fix forward against roll back.
Real world

Constructed Quarrybank example, measured on the bench. With the same plan, keeping the evidence leaves the leaked table's exposure measured at 8,400 of 18,400 customer rows. Dropping it means nobody can show which rows were fetched, so all 18,400 have to be treated as exposed, and the bad load's correction slips from 6h50 to 10h50 because the data has to be reloaded before it can be fixed.

Self-studyRetention and access for the evidence itself2 min▶

Evidence of a leaked table is itself a copy of the leaked data. NIST RS.AN-06 asks that incident records be safeguarded and that "only authorized personnel have access". Put the snapshot somewhere with its own narrow access list and a deletion date agreed with the privacy partner, and record both in the evidence section.

Build-along

Open the record for the bad load

★ 22 min · everyone builds

You are the on-call engineer at 02:40Z on 10 February. Open Quarrybank's record for the bad load, run the bench to see the times your record will have to hold, and leave with the timeline template that becomes page 1 of the responder half of the plan.

Open the record from the template. One document, named for the date and the type. Fill the status block before anything else, even if most of it says "unknown".

★ Step 1 · status block, 02:55ZINCIDENT 2026-02-10 quality · Quarrybank bad load STATUS investigating SEVERITY unset (leader sets it, session 2) OWNER on-call analytics engineer KNOWN row count doubled for 2 regions in partition 2026-02-10 NEXT UPDATE 03:30Z in #incident

Write the first four timeline rows, in UTC, with proof. Use the six fields from Part 1. The partner extract row is the one most teams leave out because nobody had noticed it yet; ask the orchestrator what ran after the load and write down everything.

★ Step 2 · first timeline rowstime who saw/did what proof 02:00Z supplier feed saw partition 2026-02-10 loaded (dup) load run id 02:30Z orchestrator saw weekly partner extract sent run id, file name 02:40Z volume monitor saw row count x2, regions North and East alert id 02:55Z on-call did declared, record opened this document

Quarantine before you fix, and write it down. Copy the partition and its load log to a protected location, revoke read on the original, and record both paths. Do not delete anything.

Run the bench, R0 then R1. In the table, read the bad load's row: under no plan it is declared 6h30 after the signal; under a declare rule, 0h15. Then read the contain and correct columns for R1, 6h15 and 12h15: those are the last two rows of your timeline, and they are slow because nobody yet owns severity or roles. Sessions 2 and 3 of the leader track fix that.

Save the template. Strip Quarrybank's specifics out of steps 1 and 2 and keep the shape. That is page 1 of the responder half of the plan.

STATUS block (rewrite every update): status / type / severity / owner / known / next update TIMELINE (append only, never edit a row; correct with a new row): time in UTC with Z | who (person or system) | saw or did | what, one fact | proof | next EVIDENCE: where the quarantined copy and logs are, who can read them, delete-by date Rule: quarantine and snapshot before any fix. Never drop the bad table.

A record opened late is reconstructed, not recorded. If you are already an hour in when you start, say so in the first row: "record opened 03:45Z, rows above reconstructed from chat and logs". Honesty about provenance is part of provenance.

Homework

Before session 2

30 min
  1. Make the timeline template real where you work: one document or ticket type that anyone on call can open in under five minutes.
  2. Find out which of your systems log in UTC and which do not. List the ones that do not; those are the rows that will be misordered.
  3. Write down how you would quarantine a bad partition in your warehouse without deleting it, and who has permission to do it at 3am.
  4. For the last real data problem you handled, try to rebuild its timeline in UTC from logs alone. Note every row you could not prove.
Check yourself

Three questions

At 02:45Z the bad partition is still being read by dashboards. What should happen to it first?

Quarantine stops the reading and keeps the evidence. On the bench, dropping the table turns a measured exposure of 8,400 rows into an assumed 18,400 and delays the correction by four hours.

A colleague wrote "10:55 declaring" in chat from an office eight hours ahead of UTC. The monitor log says 02:40. What does the record need?

One clock, with the zone written. Mixed clocks put events in the wrong order and make "did it leave before we knew?" unanswerable.

Which document does the SRE book call the incident commander's most important responsibility?

"The incident commander's most important responsibility is to keep a living incident document." The postmortem is written later, from it.
