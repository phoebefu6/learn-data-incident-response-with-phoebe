Part 0

Closing is a decision, not a feeling

Every session so far has moved an incident forward: open the record, trace the readers, stop the spread, close the doors, correct and prove it, tell everyone. Tonight is the last move a responder makes on a live incident, and it is the one most often skipped. Somebody says "I think we're done", nobody disagrees, and the incident ends without anyone being able to show why.

Live = presented in the 45 minutes Self-study = the full depth, after class 🔍 Every diagram zooms

★ What you walk out with tonight. Exit criteria for each of Quarrybank's three incident types, the list of what the record must hold before the postmortem author picks it up, a follow-up table where every row has an owner and a date, and page 7 of the responder half of the plan: the close-out record.

LiveWhy "the call went quiet" is not a close3 min▶

A quiet call means people stopped talking. It says nothing about whether a vendor still holds a copy, whether a director still has the wrong pack in their inbox, or whether the privacy partner's notice went out. The questions that arrive weeks later, from the audit committee chair, the auditor or a regulator, are all versions of one question: how did you know it was over?

The answer that holds up is a short list of criteria, agreed before the incident, each one ticked with a pointer to proof in the record. That is the whole of tonight.

Real world

Constructed Quarrybank example. After the bad load was corrected, the team marked the incident resolved in the ticket. Two weeks later the finance controller asked whether the demand forecast model had been rescored on corrected rows. Nobody could say, because "resolved" had meant "the table is fixed", and the model keeps its own copy that a rerun does not refresh.

Self-studyWho signs, and what they sign2 min▶
  • The incident lead signs the close. NIST RS.MA-01 suggests "designating an incident lead for each incident"; the person who held the incident is the person who declares its end.
  • They sign the criteria, not the mood. The signature sits under a list where every line has a proof pointer. A line without one is open.
  • Exceptions are signed too. Anything that cannot be closed by action, such as a party nobody can contact, is closed by a recorded decision with a name on it.
Part 1 · covers exit criteria, NIST RC.RP-06

Exit criteria, one set per incident type

6 min live

NIST SP 800-61 Rev. 3 states it in one line under RC.RP-06: "The end of incident recovery is declared based on criteria, and incident-related documentation is completed." Its first recommendation adds an after-action report documenting the incident, the response and recovery actions, and the lessons. Two things, then: criteria for the end, and a record complete enough to write from.

LiveQuarrybank's exit criteria by type4 min▶

The three incident types from leader session 1 each end in a different way, so each gets its own short list. Write them into the plan now, while nobody is tired.

Every type also carries two shared lines: every notice deadline met or its miss explained, and the record complete enough for the after-action report.

LiveCriteria are written before, ticked after2 min▶

Criteria written at the end of an incident tend to describe whatever the team managed to do. Criteria written in the plan describe what "safe" means, and the incident has to meet them. That is the same logic as NIST's declare rule in DE.AE-08, "Incidents are declared when adverse events meet the defined incident criteria", applied at the other end.

Real world

Constructed Quarrybank example. On the leaked table, the vendor workspace share was revoked at containment, but vendor analyst A had already downloaded a copy through it 0h30 after the bad write. "Every door closed" was true. "Every copy accounted for" was not, until the vendor sent written confirmation that the file was deleted. Two criteria, two different proofs; ticking one does not tick the other.

Self-studyWhen a criterion cannot be met2 min▶

Some criteria can never be met by action. The unknown address that paged through the leaked table's public link cannot be told, because nobody knows who it is. The close-out record does not pretend otherwise. It records the party, the rows it fetched, the fact that it is not notifiable, and the name of the person who accepted that, and it hands the "could this happen again" question to a follow-up. Whether that exposure changes what the privacy partner tells the regulator is their call, owned by the GDPR ↗ and PDPA ↗ breach response sessions.

Part 2 · covers what the record must hold for the postmortem

What the record hands the write-up

5 min live

The second half of RC.RP-06 is "incident-related documentation is completed". The responder does not write the postmortem; that craft belongs to the planned runbooks and postmortems course on the Learn with Phoebe hub ↗. The responder makes sure the author never has to reconstruct anything, because every section they need is already in the record with proof.

LiveThe five things the record must hold3 min▶
  1. The living record from responder session 1: the status block and the append-only timeline, in UTC, every row with proof.
  2. The consumer trace from session 2: every table, dashboard, extract, email, model and outside party that read the bad data, and every decision made on it.
  3. The notice list from session 6, with sent and acknowledged times for each name. Sent is your action; acknowledged is the proof it landed.
  4. The evidence locations: where the quarantined copies and logs are, who can read them, and the delete-by date agreed with the privacy partner. NIST RS.AN-06 asks that records keep "integrity and provenance", which includes knowing where they are.
  5. The bench-style numbers: consumers and decisions on bad data, never told, copies still wrong, deadlines met, and the three readiness times, declare, contain and correct, measured from the first signal.

Real world

Constructed Quarrybank example, measured on the bench. "Never told: 0" on the leaked table is true and still needs a sentence. The unknown address is on the reached list, and the bench leaves it out of "never told" because it is not notifiable. A postmortem author reading only the zero would miss it. The close-out record names it.

Self-studyWhere the record goes next2 min▶

Two places. The postmortem write-up, whose structure and tone belong to the planned runbooks and postmortems course (watch the hub ↗ for it). And the incident package that reliability keeps for each incident, covered in reliability b6 ↗. NIST adds a timing point worth keeping: lessons "should often be shared as soon as they are identified, not delayed until after recovery concludes." The close-out record collects them; it does not have to be the first place they are said.

Part 3 · covers follow-ups with owners and dates

Every follow-up has one name and one date

5 min live

A follow-up with a team as its owner is owned by nobody. A follow-up with no date is a wish. The close-out record lists every follow-up as a row with exactly one person and exactly one date, and the incident is not closed until the table is complete, even if the work itself runs for months.

LiveThe floor goes to detection3 min▶

Even the rehearsed plan leaves 5 consumers and 1 decision on bad data, measured on the bench. None of them is a response failure. On the leaked table, vendor analyst A downloaded a copy 0h30 after the bad write, and the unknown address began paging through the public link at 3h10, ten minutes before the 3h20 access alert. On the board deck, the pack and the pricing committee deck were read and "Hold prices in two regions" was decided at 24h00, a full day before the 48h00 reconciliation signal. All of those times run from the bad write.

No response plan moves an event that happened before the first signal. The follow-up for the floor is a detection follow-up, and its owner is whoever owns detection, the work covered in learn-data-observability ↗. Writing it into the close-out record is how that request reaches them with evidence attached.

Real world

Constructed Quarrybank example. The first draft of the leaked table's follow-ups said "improve monitoring, data team". The incident lead sent it back: one person, one date, one sentence of what "done" looks like. The rewrite read "alert on any new outside share of a table tagged customer, owner the observability lead, due before the next quarterly tabletop".

Self-studyFollow-ups are not blame2 min▶

The owner of a follow-up is the person who will change the system, not the person who was on call when it broke. Practitioners keep those apart on purpose: if owning a follow-up reads as fault, people stop volunteering what they saw, and the next record is thinner. The tone of the write-up itself is the docs course's ground; the close-out record only needs the names to point at work, not at people.

Homework

Before session 8

30 min
  1. Write exit criteria for the three incident types where you work, each with the proof that opens it. Keep each list to five lines or fewer.
  2. Take your last closed incident and check it against those criteria. Count the lines you could not prove from the record.
  3. Find every follow-up from that incident. Rewrite any with a team as owner, or with no date, as one name and one date.
  4. Decide who in your organisation would own a detection follow-up, and ask them whether they would accept one from an incident record.
Check yourself

Three questions

What does NIST RC.RP-06 require before the end of incident recovery is declared?

"The end of incident recovery is declared based on criteria, and incident-related documentation is completed." The after-action report follows from that record; agreement in the room is not a criterion.

On the leaked table under R5, which reached party cannot be closed by a notice, and how does the close-out record handle it?

The unknown address can never be told, and the bench leaves it out of "never told" for that reason. The record still names it, so the zero is not read as "nobody outside saw it", and its detection lesson becomes a follow-up.

The close-out record is signed. Where does the write-up go?

The responder hands over a complete record. The write-up is a separate piece of work, and only the detection follow-up goes to whoever owns monitoring.
