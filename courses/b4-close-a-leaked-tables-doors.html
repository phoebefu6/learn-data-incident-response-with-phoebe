Close a leaked table's doors

Part 0

An exposure closes door by door

Session 3 stopped bad rows travelling outward through pipelines. An exposure runs the other way: the rows sit still and people come to them, through every link, share, grant and key that points at the table. Containment for an exposure is therefore a list, and the incident is contained only when the last item on that list is shut.

Live = presented in the 45 minutes Self-study = the full depth, after class 🔍 Every diagram zooms

★ What you walk out with tonight. An exposure playbook in seven steps, with who may do each one: snapshot, list the doors, revoke them all, rotate, list the copies, request deletion, page the privacy partner. It becomes page 4 of the responder half of Quarrybank's plan, and you will see on the bench how much it changes the row count.

LiveWhy "we turned off the link" is not containment3 min▶

NIST SP 800-61 Rev. 3 defines the job plainly: "Containment refers to preventing the expansion of an incident." For a leaked table, expansion means one more person reading one more row. Turning off the link the alert named stops that through one door. Every other door keeps the incident expanding while the record says "contained".

The fix is not heroics, it is inventory. Before anyone clicks revoke, someone writes down every way the table can be reached from outside, and the revoke step is done when every line on that list has a time next to it.

Real world

Constructed Quarrybank example. At 12:20Z on 24 February an access-log alert flagged an unknown address paging through the customer table. The on-call engineer disabled the public link within minutes and wrote "contained" in the record. The vendor workspace share, set up the same morning for an analytics project, stayed open, because nobody had it on a list.

Part 1 · covers every door the table is open through

List the doors before you shut any

5 min live

Quarrybank's customer table holds 18,400 rows with contact and credit-limit columns. On the morning of 24 February it was opened two ways at once: a public link anyone holding the URL could use, and a share into the vendor's workspace for named vendor accounts. Four outside parties came through over the following day, and the alert saw only one of them.

LiveWhere doors hide on a data platform3 min▶

A door is anything that lets someone outside the response read the table without asking you first. On most warehouses and BI tools the list is longer than people expect:

  • Links: public or "anyone with the link" shares on the table, a dashboard over it, or an exported file.
  • Shares and grants: workspace shares to partners or vendors, role grants, data-sharing objects between accounts.
  • Credentials: service accounts, API keys and connection strings that can query it, including the one the vendor's notebook uses.
  • Downstream copies that are themselves open: an extract in a bucket, a cached dashboard, a sandbox someone cloned it into.
  • Agents: an AI agent or automation holding a share to the table can read it on its own schedule.

Ask the platform, not your memory: list the grants, shares and links on the object, and the credentials that touched it in the access log for the window.

LiveWho came through, and when2 min▶
Real world

Constructed Quarrybank example. Times after the share at 09:00Z. Vendor analyst A downloaded a full copy through the vendor share at 0h30, before anything looked wrong. The unknown address began paging through the public link at 3h10, ten minutes before the alert at 3h20. The person the link had been forwarded to read at 21h40. Vendor analyst B came in through the vendor share from 26h40 onward. Two of the four arrived after the signal, which means a response could have stopped them.

Part 2 · covers snapshot first, then revoke, then rotate

Snapshot, revoke every door, rotate

6 min live

The order matters because each step destroys something the next person needs. Revoking a share can reset its settings page and, on some tools, its access history. Rotating a key breaks every job using it. So the evidence goes first, then the doors all close together, then anything the exposure revealed is changed.

LiveStep one: snapshot before you change anything2 min▶

Copy the access log for the window, the share and link settings as they stand (who, what scope, created when, by whom), and the list of credentials that touched the table. Put them in the protected evidence location from session 1 and write the path in the record.

NIST RS.AN-07 is the reason this is not optional: collected incident data "is still considered evidence" even without a formal chain of custody. The access log is also the only thing that turns "every row may have leaked" into a measured number. Session 1's trap showed the cost of losing it, and the bench prints it again tonight.

LiveStep two: revoke every door, then prove each one shut2 min▶

Work down the list from Part 1. For each door write the time it closed and how you know: a failed test request through the old link, the share listing now empty, the grant gone from the role. A door is closed when a test says so, not when a button was clicked.

Real world

Constructed Quarrybank example, measured on the bench. With severity and roles but no playbook (R2), containment lands 2h35 after the signal and closes only the public link; the vendor share stays open until the correction at 8h35, and 12,800 rows end up exposed. With the containment playbook (R3), both doors close at 0h50 and the count is 8,400. The bench models these times by stated rules: a playbook makes containment faster and makes it cover every door.

LiveStep three: rotate what the exposure revealed2 min▶

Revoking stops access through a door. It does not change anything the people who came through already learned. If the share carried a service account, a connection string or an API key, or if the table itself held anything that works as a credential, it has to be rotated. The mechanics of rotation, and keeping jobs alive while you do it, belong to access control, secrets and keys ↗.

If an AI agent or automation holds the share, revoking a human's access does not stop it. Use the agent's kill switch and pull its audit record: agent secrets and access, prove what it did ↗.

Self-studyWhen the door was opened by an attacker, not a colleague2 min▶

Quarrybank's doors were opened by staff on purpose and stayed open by mistake. If the access log suggests someone created a door you did not, the incident is also a security incident: detection of exfiltration is covered in cyber security, exfiltration detection ↗, and restoring after anything was altered or destroyed in cyber security, destructive events ↗. Your sequence does not change; the security team joins the room.

Part 3 · covers what was already copied

A revoke does not recall a download

5 min live

Once every door is shut, nobody new can read the table. Everything already read is still out there. Vendor analyst A's download sits on a laptop or in a vendor bucket; the unknown address kept whatever it paged through. Containment ends the expansion; it does not shrink what already escaped. That part is a list of copies and, for each, a request.

LiveThe copies list and the deletion request3 min▶

Build the copies list from the snapshot: every download, export and query result fetched through a door before it closed, with who holds it and whether you can reach them. For each reachable holder, the request is specific: delete every copy, including derived files and backups, and confirm in writing when it is done. The confirmation goes in the evidence section of the record.

Some holders cannot be reached. The bench marks the unknown address not notifiable, and no plan changes that. Write it down as an open exposure, with what it read, so the privacy partner can weigh it.

Real world

Constructed Quarrybank example, measured on the bench. With no plan (R0) the leak reaches 4 outside parties and 2 are never told: vendor analyst B and the forwarded recipient. With a declare rule or any later plan, 2 parties are reached, vendor analyst A and the unknown address, both before or near the 3h20 signal. Those two are the floor: what happens before the first signal is detection's problem, not the responder's.

LivePage the privacy partner early, with numbers2 min▶

Customer contact and credit-limit columns are personal data, so a notice clock may be running. Under GDPR Art. 33 the controller notifies the authority "not later than 72 hours after having become aware of it", where feasible; Singapore's PDPA is reported to require notice to the PDPC no later than 3 calendar days after determining the breach is notifiable. Neither rule is yours to interpret. Your job is to hand the privacy partner, early, the inputs they need: when each door opened and closed, the rows measured exposed, who holds copies and who has confirmed deletion.

The law and the notice itself live in GDPR breach response ↗, PDPA breach response ↗ and data governance, protection and breach ↗.

Self-studyWhy a smaller number is worth the work2 min▶

18,400, 12,800 and 8,400 are three answers to the same question from the same incident. Each one changes who has to be told, what the notice says and how the board hears it. Practice among responders is to treat a measured number as the most valuable thing the exposure response produces after the closed doors themselves, because every downstream decision is sized by it.

Homework

Before session 5

30 min
  1. Pick one table with personal data where you work and list every door it is open through today: links, shares, grants, credentials, open copies, agents. Note which ones you found only by asking the platform.
  2. Find out where its access log lives, how long it is kept, and whether revoking a share on your tools erases that share's history. That answer decides how urgent step 1 is.
  3. Name who may revoke and who may rotate on that table at 3am, and check they actually have the permission. Add any gap to your roles card.
  4. Draft the deletion request you would send a vendor: what to delete, including derived files, and what written confirmation you need back. Keep it to five lines.
Check yourself

Three questions

The access-log alert has just fired on Quarrybank's customer table. Why snapshot the logs and share settings before revoking anything?

NIST RS.AN-07: collected data "is still considered evidence". Without the logs, every row must be assumed exposed: 18,400 on the bench instead of 8,400 measured.

Vendor analyst A downloaded a full copy at 0h30. The vendor share is revoked at 0h50 after the signal. What has the revoke done to A's copy?

A revoke stops new access. It does not recall a download. The copy goes on the copies list with a deletion request and a confirmation filed in the record.

On the bench R2 exposes 12,800 rows of the leaked table and R3 exposes 8,400. Why?

The playbook closes every door at containment, 0h50 after the signal. Without it the second door stays open until 8h35, and reads through it keep adding rows. Dropping the table is trap T2, which makes the number worse.
