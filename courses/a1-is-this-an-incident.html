Learn Incident Response for Data Teams with Phoebe · Leader session 1 of 6

Is this an incident yet?

Every data team has had the morning where the numbers were wrong for hours before anyone said the word "incident". Tonight is about that word: what it covers for a data team, why the moment you say it matters more than almost anything you do after, and a declare rule you write down before you need it. The running case is Quarrybank, a constructed building-supplies distributor with three incidents in one quarter, and the bench replays all three so you can see what waiting for a complaint costs.

Part 0

How the leader track works

Six sessions, 45 minutes each, one constructed company. Quarrybank distributes building supplies from a cloud warehouse that feeds about 40 dashboards, a weekly extract to a supplier partner and a monthly board pack. In one quarter it has a bad load, a leaked table and a wrong number on the board deck. Tonight decides when each of those becomes an incident. Session 2 rates how bad each one is, session 3 staffs the room, session 4 writes the updates, session 5 handles the number the board already used, and session 6 rehearses the whole plan. The responder track runs alongside and does the hands-on work: the timeline, the trace, the containment and the notices.

Live = presented in the 45 minutes Self-study = the full depth, after class 🔍 Every diagram zooms

★ What you walk out with tonight. A definition of a data incident that covers more than security, three types you can name in a meeting, and page 1 of Quarrybank's incident response plan: a declare rule that fires on the first signal instead of the first complaint. On the bench, that one rule moves Quarrybank's quarter from 14 consumers and 7 decisions using bad data to 10 and 4.

LiveWhy the word matters more than the fix3 min▶

A problem with no name is handled by whoever happens to notice it, in whatever channel they happen to be in, with nobody keeping time. An incident is a problem with a name, an owner and a clock. Saying the word is what turns on everything else: the severity, the roles, the updates, the notices. Nothing in the rest of the track works until somebody has said it.

The cost of not saying it is not the fix taking longer. Data spreads while nobody owns it. Every hour a bad table sits in the warehouse it is copied into another dashboard, emailed to another inbox, trained into another model or quoted in another decision, and each of those copies becomes a separate person you will eventually have to tell.

Real world

Constructed Quarrybank example. On the night of the bad load a volume monitor fired 40 minutes after the duplicated partition landed. Nobody declared anything. At 7 hours 10 minutes a regional sales head emailed to ask why revenue had doubled, and that email is what started the response. In between, the bad numbers reached a sales email to 40 people, the partner extract and a forecast model.

Self-studyThe Quarrybank cast3 min▶
  • The head of data owns the warehouse and its outputs and is the person who usually decides whether something is an incident. Tonight is mostly for this person.
  • The privacy partner sits in legal and owns the breach-notice clocks. Session 4 gives this person a seat at the table from the first hour.
  • The finance controller owns the board pack and the margin figure. Session 5 is the conversation this person dreads.
  • The on-call analytics engineer sees the first signal and does the hands-on work. The responder track is written for this person.
  • The consumers: regional managers on dashboards, 40 people on a daily sales email, a supplier partner, a forecast model, the board. They are the ones the incident is about.
Part 1 · covers the NIST definition and three data incident types

Three ways data goes wrong that are worth a name

6 min live

NIST's incident response guidance, revised in April 2025, defines an incident as an occurrence that jeopardises "the integrity, confidentiality, or availability of information". It is written for cybersecurity and says so. A data team can borrow the shape without the attacker: integrity is a bad load, confidentiality is a leaked table, and a third kind exists that security frameworks rarely name, where the data was only wrong for a while but somebody decided something on it.

LiveWhat NIST says an incident is, and the part a data team adds3 min▶

NIST SP 800-61 Revision 3, published in April 2025 and now aligned to the Cybersecurity Framework 2.0, takes its definition from US federal law: an incident is "an occurrence that actually or imminently jeopardizes, without lawful authority, the integrity, confidentiality, or availability of information or an information system". It is explicit about scope: "This guide addresses only adverse cybersecurity events."

A data team needs the same three words and fewer assumptions about an attacker. A supplier feed that duplicates a partition has nobody acting without lawful authority, and the revenue figure is still unfit to read. So the working definition at Quarrybank keeps NIST's structure and drops the attacker: an incident is an event that has made data wrong, missing, or visible to the wrong people, and has reached, or could reach, someone who will use it before it is fixed.

Borrow the security plumbing, not the security mindset. If your organisation already has a security incident process, run data incidents through the same declare, severity and roles machinery. Most of what follows is the same. The difference is who you tell and what counts as fixed.

The types are not exclusive. Quarrybank's board incident is a quality incident that was found late enough to become a decision incident. Naming the type is still worth it, because it decides who joins the call: an exposure needs the privacy partner in the first hour, a decision incident needs the finance controller, and a quality incident needs whoever owns the copies.

Part 2 · covers declaring on criteria, the SRE book's three questions, declare early

A declare rule, written before you need it

6 min live

NIST lists it as a single outcome: "Incidents are declared when adverse events meet the defined incident criteria." The word that matters is defined. A team without written criteria declares when somebody senior is annoyed enough, which in practice means when a consumer complains, which means after the data has already spread.

LiveThe SRE book's three questions, adapted for data3 min▶

Google's SRE book gives a test short enough to remember at 3am. If any of these is true, it is an incident: "Do you need to involve a second team in fixing the problem?", "Is the outage visible to customers?", "Is the issue unsolved even after an hour's concentrated analysis?"

For a data team the second question needs translating, because the customer of a table is whoever reads it. Quarrybank's adapted rule:

  1. Has bad or exposed data reached a consumer, or could it reach one before you can fix it? A dashboard, an extract, an email, a model, a person outside.
  2. Does fixing it need a second team, such as the partner's integration team, legal, or finance?
  3. Is it unexplained after an hour of somebody looking at it properly?

Any yes, declare. The first question does most of the work, and it turns on the word "could": the bad load had reached the partner extract 10 minutes before the monitor fired.

LiveDeclare early, close early3 min▶

Both Google sources say the same thing in different words. The SRE book: "It is better to declare an incident early and then find a simple fix and close out the incident." The SRE workbook lists "Declare incidents early and often" as a basic principle and reports that "managed incidents are resolved faster".

The reason is structural, not motivational. Declaring is what assigns an owner and starts a timeline. An incident declared at 0h55 and closed at 1h30 as a false alarm costs one person 35 minutes and one log entry. An incident declared at 7h10 costs every hour in between, spent by the data, spreading.

Real world

Constructed Quarrybank example, measured on the bench. With no declare rule the response to the three incidents starts at the first complaint: 6h30 after the first signal for the bad load, 45h00 for the leaked table and 120h00 for the board deck. A rule that declares 15 minutes after the first signal brings all three to 0h15.

Part 3 · covers the cost of declaring late against the cost of declaring often

Late costs data, often costs attention

5 min live

The objection to a low threshold is real: a team that declares everything learns to ignore its own declarations. The answer is not a higher threshold. It is making a declaration cheap to make and cheap to close, and letting severity, next session, decide how much of the organisation wakes up.

LiveMaking a declaration cheap3 min▶
  • Anyone can declare. The on-call engineer who sees the signal, not their manager. A rule that needs permission to fire is a rule that fires at the complaint.
  • Declaring is one action. A channel, a record, an owner. Not a meeting.
  • Closing a false alarm is praised. If the person who declared a non-incident hears about it in a performance review, the next one will not be declared.
  • Severity carries the weight. A low-severity declaration wakes nobody else. Session 2 builds the matrix that makes this safe.
Self-studyWhat the declare rule does not decide3 min▶

A declaration is not a breach notification. Whether the leaked table must be reported to a regulator, and by when, is a legal question with its own clock and its own owner, the privacy partner. The GDPR and PDPA courses own that law (GDPR breach response ↗, PDPA breach response ↗). What the declare rule does is make sure the privacy partner hears about it while there is still time on that clock.

A declaration is also not detection. How the volume monitor knew the row count had doubled is the observability course's subject (Data Observability ↗). Here the signal is an input, and the question is only what you do in the minutes after it.

Build-along

Classify, then write the rule that would have fired

★ 22 min · everyone builds

No code. Classify Quarrybank's three incidents, run the bench with and without a declare rule, and write page 1 of the incident response plan. Then do the same for one real event from your own last quarter.

Classify the three. For each Quarrybank incident write its type (quality, exposure, decision), what travels, and the first consumer it reaches. Use the three panels in Part 1. The board deck is a quality incident found late enough to be a decision incident: write both, and say which one decides who joins the call.

Run the bench on R0, no plan. Read the headline: consumers and decisions that used bad data before it was corrected or they were told. Then read the table: how long after the first signal each incident was declared. Note the three declare times.

Switch to R1, declare rule. Same quarter, same events, one change: each incident is declared 15 minutes after its first signal. Watch the headline fall from 14 consumers and 7 decisions to 10 and 4, and the never-told count from 11 to 7. Notice what does not move: deadlines met stays at 0 of 4. Declaring early starts the clock; it does not staff the room. That is session 3.

Test it on your own quarter. Pick one event from the last three months that was handled without being called an incident. Run it through the three questions. If any answer is yes, write down the time the rule would have fired and the time the response actually started.

✕ The declare rule most teams actually haveWe will declare an incident when the issue is significant and impacts the business.

"Significant" is the word that waits for a complaint. Nobody can tell at 0h40 whether a doubled row count is significant. Everybody can tell whether it has reached a dashboard.

Check yourself

Three questions

A vendor was given a public link to a customer table. Every row in it is correct. What kind of incident is it?

Confidentiality is one of the three properties in NIST's definition. Correct data in the wrong hands is an incident, and it is the type that needs the privacy partner in the first hour.

On the bench, what does adding a declare rule (R1) change for Quarrybank's quarter, compared with no plan (R0)?

Declaring 15 minutes after the first signal stops some of the spread. Meeting deadlines needs someone who owns them, which arrives with roles in session 3.

Why does the SRE book prefer declaring early even if it turns out to be minor?

Declaring assigns an owner and starts the timeline. Severity is a separate decision, and legal deadlines belong to the privacy partner, not to the declare rule.
