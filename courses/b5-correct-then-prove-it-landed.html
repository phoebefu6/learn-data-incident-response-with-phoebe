Correct, then prove it landed

The bad partition is quarantined, the valves are shut, and someone asks the question everyone has been waiting to ask: is it fixed yet? The honest answer is never "the table is fixed". A correction is done when every consumer on the trace reads corrected rows, and some of those consumers are an email in forty inboxes and a file sitting at a partner. Tonight you choose between fixing forward and rolling back, sort what a rerun clears from what it never reaches, and write a correction check that carries one row of proof per consumer.

Part 0

Fixed is a property of the readers

Sessions 1 to 4 kept the evidence, traced who read the bad data and stopped it travelling. Tonight is the part people usually mean by "the fix", and the part teams most often declare done too early. The rows in the warehouse are the easy half. The hard half is every place those rows were read, copied, sent or used since 02:00Z on 10 February, and showing, consumer by consumer, that each one now reads the corrected version.

LiveWhy "the table is fixed" is the wrong finish line3 min▶

A fixed table is a claim about one object. The incident was never about one object: session 2's trace lists dashboards, a mart, a model, an email and a partner file, and each of them read the bad rows on its own schedule. Some will pick up the correction the next time they refresh. Some never will, because the copy has already left the warehouse and nothing you rerun can reach it.

So the finish line is written per consumer. The leader asking "is it fixed?" deserves an answer like "four of six consumers proven on corrected rows, the partner extract waiting on their reload, the sales email recipients told at the time in the record". That sentence is only possible if the check exists before anyone asks.

Real world

Constructed Quarrybank example. The rerun finished, the revenue dashboard looked right, and the on-call engineer posted "fixed" in the incident channel. Two days later the forecast model's owner asked why the replenishment numbers still assumed doubled revenue in two regions. The model had run on the bad partition before anyone was awake, and nobody had rerun it or told its owner.

Part 1 · covers fix forward vs roll back

Fix forward or roll back, and keep the bad version either way

5 min live

There are two ways from a quarantined bad partition to a corrected state. Fix forward: write the corrected rows as a new version and let everything downstream rerun from it. Roll back: restore the last good version, through warehouse time travel or a versioned table, and reload the bad day cleanly afterwards. Neither is always right. The choice is a judgement about what else has changed since the bad write and how far the bad load has spread.

LiveThe trade-off, in four questions3 min▶
AskLeans fix forwardLeans roll back
What else has changed in the table since the bad write?Good loads landed after it and must surviveNothing, or nothing anyone needs yet
Can you restore a known good version?No versioning, or the retention window has passedTime travel or a versioned table holds the last good state
How far did the bad load spread?Many consumers, so one clean new version to rerun from is simplerFew consumers, and they all read the restored table
How sure are you of the corrected rows?You know exactly what was wrong and can rebuild itYou do not yet, and the last good state is safer than a guess

There is no score that settles it. Write the answer to each question in the record next to the decision and who made it, so the choice can be checked later rather than remembered.

LiveThe bad version stays, whichever way you go2 min▶

NIST notes that collected incident data "is still considered evidence" even without formal chain of custody (RS.AN-07). The quarantined v2 is how you will later show which rows were wrong and what each consumer read. A roll back that overwrites it, or a fix forward that "cleans up" the quarantine schema, deletes the evidence on the way to the correction. Keep v2 until the incident is closed, with a delete-by date agreed in the record.

Real world

Constructed Quarrybank example. The bad load doubled revenue for two regions, and a good load for 11 February landed on top of it before anyone noticed. Rolling back to 9 February would have thrown that good day away, so the team fixed forward: corrected rows for 10 February written as a new version, the quarantined partition left untouched, and downstream rerun from the new version.

Self-studyWhat belongs to reliability, and what to cyber2 min▶

How to rebuild a partition and rerun what depends on it so that a second run does not double the rows again is backfill and idempotent rerun mechanics. It belongs to reliability, and is not retaught here: Learn Data Reliability, backfills ↗. Restoring after a destructive event, where an attacker deleted or encrypted the data, is a security restore with its own concerns: Learn Cyber Security, destructive events ↗. Tonight is the decision and the proof around both.

Warehouse time travel and versioned table formats are vendor features with their own retention rules. Treat any vendor claim about how far back you can restore as a claim to check against your own settings before you rely on it at 3am.

Part 2 · covers why refreshable outputs and copies behave differently

A rerun refreshes tables, never inboxes

5 min live

Every consumer on the trace falls into one of two kinds. Dashboards and tables read the warehouse when they refresh, so the correction reaches them the next time they do. Copies were made once and live somewhere else: an email, an extract, a model's output, a deck. A copy only clears when someone tells the people holding it. The bench applies exactly this rule.

LiveThe sorting rule, across all three incidents3 min▶
Refreshes at the correctionOnly clears when its users are told
Revenue dashboardSales email
Executive dashboardPartner extract
Finance martForecast model
Board portal copyEmailed board pack, pricing deck, investor update, lender certificate

The test is simple: does the consumer read the warehouse again on its own, or was it made once and handed to someone? A forecast model sits on the copy side even though it lives in your stack, because its last run has already fed numbers to people who acted on them. Rerunning the model makes a new correct output; it does not change the order that was placed from the old one.

LiveWhat the bench says about copies2 min▶

Measured on the bench, across the quarter's three incidents, the copies still wrong at the end are 7 with no plan, 4 under the containment playbook (R3) and 0 under the lineage-driven consumer notice (R4). R3 and R4 correct at the same time, 6h50 after the first signal. The difference is not a faster fix. It is that R4 tells the holder of every copy on the trace, including the partner extract and the forecast model from the bad load, which R3 leaves never told.

Real world

Constructed Quarrybank example, measured on the bench. Under R3 the warehouse was correct by 09:30Z on 10 February, and the partner was still working from a weekly extract with revenue doubled for two regions. The partner contract notice was missed. Under R4 the same correction, at the same time, ended with no copy still wrong, because the partner was told 2h00 after the bad write, measured from the bad write against a due time of 24h40.

Self-studyRecall is a notice with extra steps2 min▶

Some email tools offer to recall a message, and some partners will replace a file if asked. Both still depend on a person on the other side acting. Record them as a notice with an expected action, not as a correction you performed, and keep the copy row open until someone confirms the replacement. Writing the notice itself is session 6.

Part 3 · covers proving it landed, NIST RC.RP-05

Proof per consumer, or it did not land

5 min live

NIST's recovery guidance asks you to "Verify the correctness and adequacy of the restoration actions taken before putting a restored system online." For a data incident the restored system is not one table. It is every consumer on the trace. The correction check is that verification written down: for each consumer, a query or a look that shows it now reads corrected rows, with its UTC time and its proof in the record.

LiveWhat counts as proof3 min▶
  • For a table or mart: a query that would have failed on the bad rows and passes now, such as a duplicate key count of zero or a regional total that matches the corrected source. Record the query id, not "looked fine".
  • For a dashboard: the tile's value next to the query that should produce it, with a screenshot stamped in UTC. Dashboards cache; a value checked before the cache refreshed proves nothing.
  • For a copy: the notice that went to its holders, and where possible their confirmation that the old copy was replaced. Until then the row stays open.
  • For a model: the run id of the rerun on corrected rows, plus the notice to the owner about the outputs already used.

Check from the consumer's side. A query against the corrected table proves the table. Only a check that goes through the consumer, the dashboard's own tile, the mart's own output, proves the consumer reads it.

LiveCorrection times on the bench2 min▶

Measured on the bench, from the first signal: R3 and R4 correct at 6h50, the rehearsed plan R5 at 3h25, and T2, the plan that dropped the bad table, at 10h50. T2 is the same plan as R4 with one bad habit, and the bench's rules charge 240 extra minutes when the table was dropped, because the data has to be reloaded before it can be corrected. The bench models these times by stated rules; they show the order of the plans, not a promise about your warehouse.

Real world

Constructed Quarrybank example, measured on the bench. The trap switch on the rehearsed plan tells the same story: R5 corrects at 3h25, and the same plan with the bad table dropped corrects at 7h25. Practice buys hours; deleting the evidence gives four of them back.

Self-studyWho signs the check2 min▶

As practice, the person who ran the correction should not be the only one who ticks the rows. A second responder, or the owner of each consumer, runs the check from their side. The incident commander does not tick rows either: PagerDuty's guide is explicit that "the Incident Commander is NOT a resolver". The commander reads the finished check and decides whether the correction is done, which feeds the exit criteria in session 7.

Build-along

Write the correction check for the bad load

★ 22 min · everyone builds

You are the responder who ran the correction for Quarrybank's bad load. Read on the bench how long each plan takes to correct and how many copies it leaves wrong, then write the check that proves your correction landed on every consumer, and save its shape as page 5 of the responder half of the plan.

Read R3 and R4 side by side. On the bench, find the correct column: both read 6h50 from the first signal. Then find copies still wrong: R3 4, R4 0. Write one line in your record explaining the difference in your own words. If your line mentions a faster fix, read Part 2 again.

Switch to R5, then T2. R5 corrects at 3h25; T2 at 10h50, both from the first signal. Note that T2 still leaves 0 copies wrong: dropping the table did not make the notice worse, it made the correction later and the exposure assumed rather than measured, as session 4 showed.

Copy one row per consumer from your session 2 trace. For the bad load that is the revenue dashboard, executive dashboard, finance mart, sales email, partner extract and forecast model. Mark each one "refreshes" or "copy" using the test from Part 2.

Homework

Before session 6

30 min

Check yourself

Three questions

The finance mart has been rerun on corrected rows. Why is the sales email still wrong?

Refreshable outputs read the warehouse again; copies were made once and handed to people. On the bench, copies still wrong at the end are R3 4 and R4 0 with the same correction time: the difference is telling the holders.

What does NIST RC.RP-05 ask for before a restored system goes back online?

"Verify the correctness and adequacy of the restoration actions taken before putting a restored system online." For data, the correction check is that verification, one consumer at a time.

Why does T2 correct at 10h50 after the first signal, when R4 corrects at 6h50?

T2 is the R4 plan with the bad table dropped. The bench charges 240 extra minutes for the reload, and the evidence of which rows were wrong is gone too.
