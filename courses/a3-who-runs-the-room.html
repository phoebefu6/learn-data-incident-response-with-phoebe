Who runs the room

You have declared it and you have sized it. Now twelve people join a call, four of them start typing into the warehouse, the most senior person starts asking for updates, and nobody is writing anything down. Tonight is about the handful of roles that turn that call into a response: one person who holds the picture and fixes nothing, one pair of hands on the system, one person telling the outside world, one person writing it all down. Quarrybank's bench shows what those roles are worth in deadlines.

Part 0

Roles inside the incident, not the rota

Two different questions hide behind "who is responsible". The first is who gets paged at 3am this week, which is a rota, and the reliability course designs it (On-call is a design ↗). The second is what each person does once the incident is declared and the call has started. That second question is tonight. A good rota gets the right person awake; only roles decide whether the next six hours are a response or a crowd.

LiveThe call nobody was running3 min▶

Most chaotic incident calls are not short of skill. They are short of structure. Everyone on the call is competent and everyone is doing something useful in isolation: one person is querying the bad partition, another is rerunning a job, a third is drafting a message to the partner, a fourth is asking the first three for a status. The work collides because nobody is deciding which of those things happens first, and nobody is keeping the record that would let anyone check later.

Roles fix that by narrowing each person's job. The narrowing is the point: a commander who also debugs loses the picture, and an engineer who also writes the executive update loses an hour of fixing.

Real world

Constructed Quarrybank example. When the customer table was found behind a public link, the head of data declared within 15 minutes of the access-log alert. With a declare rule but no roles, the next four hours went on everyone looking at the access logs together. Nobody had been told that assessing the exposure was their job, and the privacy partner heard about it only when legal was copied into a thread days later.

Part 1 · covers the SRE book's incident command roles and PagerDuty's roles

Four jobs, one hub

6 min live

Google's SRE book splits an incident into four jobs and gives each a one-line definition worth memorising. Incident Command "holds the high-level state about the incident". Operational Work is "the only group modifying the system during an incident". Communication is "the public face of the incident response task force". Planning "supports Ops by dealing with longer-term issues". Everything else on tonight's page is a variation on those four sentences.

LiveThe SRE book's four roles, mapped to Quarrybank3 min▶
RoleSRE book, in its wordsQuarrybank
Incident Command"holds the high-level state about the incident"The head of data, by default, unless the rota says otherwise
Operational Work"the only group modifying the system during an incident"The on-call analytics engineer, plus anyone the commander adds to that group by name
Communication"the public face of the incident response task force"The privacy partner for anything leaving the building; the commander's own updates inside it
Planning"supports Ops by dealing with longer-term issues"The finance controller on a decision incident; otherwise a second engineer filing follow-ups

The word "only" in the operations line carries the most weight. A data incident has a special way of getting worse: two people fixing the same table. One reruns the load while the other deletes the duplicated partition, and the evidence of what was wrong disappears between them.

The SRE workbook adds the rule that makes this work on a small team: "By default, the IC assumes all roles that have not been delegated yet." On a quiet night that means one person holds everything. The commander's job is then to give roles away as soon as a second person arrives, starting with operations, so their own hands come off the keyboard.

LivePagerDuty's roles, and what NIST asks for3 min▶

PagerDuty's public incident response documentation names a slightly longer list: Incident Commander, Deputy, Scribe, Subject Matter Expert, Customer Liaison and Internal Liaison. The Scribe "documents the timeline of an incident as it progresses". The Deputy backs up the commander and takes over if needed. The Subject Matter Experts are the people who actually fix things. The two liaisons split the SRE book's communication role into an outward face and an inward one.

For a data team, the split is useful. Quarrybank's outward voice on the leaked table must be the privacy partner, because what is said to a regulator or a vendor has legal weight. The inward voice, to regional managers and the 40 people on the sales email, can be the commander or a delegate.

NIST's guidance asks for less, and it asks it quietly. RS.MA-01 C1: "Consider designating an incident lead for each incident." One named lead is the floor. Everything above is how you scale that lead when one person cannot hold it.

Name roles, not titles. The roles card says "commander", not "head of data". The head of data is the default holder, and the card says how command passes when they are asleep, on leave, or the cause of the incident.

Part 2 · covers why the most senior person should not run it

Rank is not command

5 min live

PagerDuty's incident commander training says it bluntly: the commander becomes "the highest ranking individual on any major incident call, regardless of their day-to-day rank." It goes further: the commander may remove anyone disruptive from the call "(even if it's the CEO)". The instinct on most data teams runs the other way. The most senior person joins, starts asking questions, and the call reorganises itself around answering them.

LiveWhat the commander does with repair work3 min▶

PagerDuty's roles page has the shortest rule on the subject: "Delegate all repair actions, the Incident Commander is NOT a resolver." The capital letters are theirs, and they are there because the temptation is constant. The commander usually knows the system well, often better than the engineer at the keyboard. The moment they open a query window, though, the picture they were holding stops being updated, and the call has no centre.

The training also says what the commander does instead: decide. "Making the "wrong" decision is better than making no decision." On a data call the decisions are concrete: pause the partner extract or not, revoke the public link now or after a snapshot, tell the 40 sales-email recipients tonight or in the morning. A commander who decides and logs the reason can be corrected later. A call that never decides keeps spreading the data while it debates.

Real world

Constructed Quarrybank example. On the bad load, the CEO joined the call within the hour, as most leaders would. In the version without roles, the call became a series of answers to the CEO's questions while three people queried the same partition. In the version with roles, the head of data, as commander, gave the CEO a two-line status, named the on-call engineer as the only person touching the warehouse, and asked the CEO to hold questions for the next scheduled update.

LiveThe honest disagreement: rank versus skill2 min▶

None of this means command never moves to someone more senior. PagerDuty's training is careful: "the arrival of a more qualified person does NOT necessarily mean a change in incident command." Not necessarily is not never. And the SRE workbook's own case study shows command passing to a more experienced SRE during the incident, deliberately, because their experience was what the incident needed.

So the two sources are not saying the same thing, and it is worth being exact about where they differ. PagerDuty's rule is about rank: a title does not take command. The workbook shows that experience can be a good reason to hand command over. The workbook does not set a rule about seniority either way. Read together, the working norm is: command can move for skill, through an explicit handover, and never because someone important has joined the call.

Give your CEO a seat, not the chair. Before the next incident, tell senior leaders what they will get: a status at a set cadence and a named commander. Most senior people interrupt a call because they do not know when the next update is coming.

Part 3 · covers one owner and an explicit handover

One commander at a time, handed over out loud

4 min live

An incident that runs past a working shift will change commanders, and the handover is where the picture is most often lost. The SRE book's rule is simple: the outgoing commander stays in command until the incoming one acknowledges, out loud, with a sentence like "You're now the incident commander, okay?" Until that answer comes back, the old commander is still the commander.

LiveWhy the acknowledgement is the whole trick2 min▶

The failure the acknowledgement prevents is quiet. The outgoing commander assumes the briefing was the handover and goes to bed. The incoming one assumes they are still being briefed and waits. For twenty minutes nobody is deciding anything, and on a data incident that is twenty minutes of the bad table being read. A single spoken question and a spoken yes closes the gap, and the scribe writing the time down makes it checkable afterwards.

The SRE workbook's case study also shows the rhythm: the team rotated "on-call engineers and the IC every four hours". Long incidents are a stamina problem as much as a skill problem. A tired commander makes slower decisions, and a planned four-hour rotation means the handover is a routine rather than an emergency.

Self-studyOne owner, from declaration to close3 min▶
  • One commander at a time. Not a commander and a "co-lead". Two people who both think they decide is the same as nobody deciding.
  • The deputy is the default next commander. PagerDuty's Deputy role exists so the handover has a named destination before anyone is tired.
  • The commander can be the cause. If the head of data approved the vendor share that leaked the table, command passes to the deputy at declaration. That is not a punishment; it removes a conflict from the call.
  • Close is a handover too. When the incident closes, the commander hands follow-up actions to named owners. Writing up the incident belongs to the planned runbooks and postmortems course (Learn with Phoebe hub ↗).
Build-along

Staff the room, then watch the deadlines move

★ 22 min · everyone builds

No code. Run the bench with a declare rule only, then with severity and roles, and read why the deadlines change. Then fill Quarrybank's roles card for the leaked table, page 3 of its incident response plan, and do the same for your own team.

Run the bench on R1, declare rule only. This is where session 1 left Quarrybank. Read the headline: 10 consumers and 4 decisions used bad data, 7 consumers were never told, and 0 of 4 notice deadlines were met. Note the containment time in the table: 6h15 after the first signal on all three incidents.

Switch to R2, severity and roles. Same quarter, same events. The headline falls to 7 consumers and 3 decisions, never told falls from 7 to 4, deadlines met rise from 0 of 4 to 3 of 4, and containment moves from 6h15 to 2h35 after the first signal. People who read bad data fall from 62 to 17.

Explain the move from the bench's rules, not from intuition. The bench models plan times by stated rules. Without roles, assessment happens 240 minutes after declaring, because nobody has been told it is their job; with roles it happens 20 minutes after. Containment follows assessment by 120 minutes in both rungs, so the whole saving is the assessment gap. The deadlines move for a different reason: with roles, the privacy partner owns the formal notice and sends it within 24 hours of assessment; without roles it waits until legal finds out, 120 hours after declaring. That is the difference between the leaked table missing both the GDPR and PDPA deadlines and meeting both.

Fill it for your own team. Copy the card and replace every name with a real person on your team. Any row where the honest answer is "the same person as the commander" is a role that will not be done during your next incident.

The roles card most teams actually haveWhoever is most senior on the call leads. Everyone helps fix it. We will update stakeholders when we know more.

"Everyone helps fix it" is how a leaked table loses its evidence. Two people revoking access in two different ways, with nobody writing down which door was closed when, leaves you unable to say what was exposed for how long.

Homework

Before session 4

30 min
  1. Finish your own roles card and show it to the person who would be your deputy. Ask them which handover step they have never actually done.
  2. Tell the two most senior people likely to join your next incident call what they will get from it: a named commander and a status at a set cadence. Write down their reaction.
  3. Find the person who owns breach notification in your organisation, the one you named in session 1, and ask whether they would want to be on the call in the first hour. Put their answer on the card.
  4. Look back at your last incident call or chat thread. Count how many people changed the system during it. If the answer is more than one, write down who decided that.
Check yourself

Three questions

The CEO joins Quarrybank's incident call about the bad load. Should the CEO take over running it?

PagerDuty's commander training is explicit, and it even allows removing the CEO from the call. Command can still move to someone more experienced, as in the SRE workbook's case study, but by handover and acknowledgement, not by arrival.

The commander knows exactly which query would find the duplicated partition. What should they do with that repair work?

A commander at the keyboard stops updating the picture. Two people modifying the system at once is the failure the SRE book's "only group modifying the system" rule exists to prevent.

On the bench, what happens to deadlines met when Quarrybank moves from R1 (declare rule) to R2 (severity and roles)?

The partner's contract notice on the bad load is still missed on R2: the notice has an owner, but nobody has traced which consumers read the bad rows. Lineage closes that gap later.
